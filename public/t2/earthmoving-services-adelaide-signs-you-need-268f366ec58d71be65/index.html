Simply Earthworks frames earthmoving as a scope-defining exercise rather than solely a machinery-based decision. The initial brief should articulate the required final ground condition, such as a prepared platform, a specific fall, a cleared zone, or a particular material outcome. This provides a clear handoff point for subsequent trades or the property owner. The same task can vary significantly in complexity based on the specified finish. Activities like land shaping, excavation to a precise depth, installing service trenches, and carting away spoil each carry distinct responsibilities. A general request for 'digging' can obscure crucial decisions about final levels, material management, site reinstatement, and who ultimately accepts the completed profile. It is vital to name the physical result, not just the action itself. Include any known supplied levels, dimensions, or required falls. Differentiate between observed site conditions and elements that still require locating, testing, or design input. Clearly state whether the next phase involves building, landscaping, drainage, or another trade.

Measure Access Before Discussing Plant

Access extends beyond simply the width of a gate. The guidance recommends detailing the entire route from the street to the work face. This includes considerations like gates, turns, eaves, gradients, surface conditions, overhead clearances, space for loading, and any features that must be preserved. A machine that can fit through an initial opening does not guarantee it can operate, manoeuvre, load, or transfer spoil effectively at the actual work site. Locality plays a practical role here. Sites in Adelaide's CBD, inner suburbs, coastal areas, foothills, or northern growth corridors present unique challenges regarding access, neighbours, runoff, and staging. These settings do not inherently determine soil type, rock presence, groundwater levels, contamination issues, or underground assets, but they shape the critical questions that need answering before pricing can occur. While photos are helpful, precise measurements significantly reduce uncertainty. Even a tight-access yard can be assessed if the route, nearby structures, material expectations, and the desired outcome are communicated early.

Choose the Scope by Responsibility

The scope of earthmoving projects can be separated into six key decision areas to ensure the client understands their specific needs:

  • Earth works: Shaping and preparing land according to agreed levels, falls, and cut-and-fill assumptions, preparing for the next stage.
  • Excavation: Removing ground to specified dimensions or depths, considering access, underground services, and spoil management.
  • Site cuts and land levelling: Establishing supplied levels and falls for building foundations, landscaping, or access construction.
  • Trenching and drainage: Focusing on narrow routes for services or drainage, following nominated lines and grades for licensed trades.
  • Soil and spoil removal: Managing the stated material, including loading, transport, and lawful disposal or receiving assumptions.
  • Tight-access work: Addressing projects with constrained openings, turns, overhead limits, and specific spoil transfer requirements.

What a Quote Should Make Visible

A comprehensive quote should reveal the underlying assumptions regarding plant, access, ground conditions, material handling, and project completion, not just present a total cost. For plant and access, clarify the equipment category, necessary attachments, mobilisation costs, and the planned operating route. Any changes to the work face or loading points may necessitate a quote review. Regarding ground conditions and quantities, key inputs include supplied dimensions, observed site conditions, allowances for rock or unsuitable materials, and the triggers for potential variations. For material handling, specify whether spoil will be separated, stockpiled, loaded, transported, sent to a specific site, or balanced with imported fill. Completion criteria must also be defined. The quote should detail the achieved profile, whether testing or survey evidence is required, what reinstatement is included, and which party is responsible for the final handoff. Without these details, quotes for the same job can appear comparable but allow for vastly different outcomes.

  1. Define the Finish. Clearly state the desired end ground condition (e.g., platform, fall, cleared zone).

  3. Separate Facts from Assumptions. Distinguish between known site conditions and elements requiring further investigation.
  4. Specify Material Handling. Outline what will happen to excavated material - removal, stockpiling, or reuse.
  5. Gather Site-Specific Information. Collect details like suburb, job type, approximate area/depth, material expectations, and photos.
