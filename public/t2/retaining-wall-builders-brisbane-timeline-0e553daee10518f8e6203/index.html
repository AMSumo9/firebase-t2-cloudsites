Loads, Water and Approval Questions

A retaining wall manages three pressures simultaneously: soil weight, water moving through the soil, and anything sitting on the ground above. A driveway, carport slab, pool shell, or neighbouring shed may act as a surcharge load near the wall. Water needs an identified path. Wall movement is often linked to redirected downpipes, washed-out fines, and dips forming above the retained area. A drainage scope should show clean aggregate, filter fabric suited to the soil, a graded subsoil line, and a lawful, visible outlet.

Approval is not solely a height question. Brisbane City Council may set limited circumstances where a wall avoids building approval, while the Queensland Development Code supplies deemed-to-satisfy conditions. Surcharge loads, nearby walls or buildings, boundary position, easements, overlays, and protected vegetation can each change the approval pathway.

Material Choice as a Site Response

Concrete sleeper, timber sleeper, reinforced block, sandstone or boulder, and gabion systems should be compared against the site problem. The decision prioritises load, footprint, drainage, and access first, then finish. Concrete sleeper systems are suitable for replacements, boundaries, and cut-and-fill projects where post embedment, access, and drainage are assessed together. Timber sleepers suit lower garden walls and terraces where member selection, moisture exposure, drainage, and maintenance are explicit. Reinforced block suits engineered loads, tighter footprints, and finished residential walls needing coordinated footing and drainage details. Natural stone and gabion options depend heavily on ground and access. Sandstone and boulder walls rely on bearing, block geometry, machinery access, and runoff management. Gabion baskets need foundation preparation, filter separation, and basket specification.

What to Put in the Written Scope

A useful enquiry provides enough information for a contractor or designer to see constraints before attending the site. A clear written scope records dimensions, symptoms, access, and nearby structures. It should also identify whether the job is a new wall, repair, replacement, or drainage investigation. Ask how the quote treats excavation, spoil, footing preparation, backfill, filter fabric, subsoil drainage, outlet location, surface water, and reinstatement. If design or approval is required, ask who prepares the design, who inspects the work, and what documentation will be supplied. Where regulated building work applies above the statutory value threshold, the contractor should hold a QBCC licence covering the class of work. Confirm whether the quoted height is visible height or retained height. Ask where collected water will discharge and how the outlet can be inspected later. Check whether nearby loads or structures have been allowed for. Use the public QBCC licence search before paying a deposit.

Common questions

What is retained height? Retained height is the vertical distance from the base of the retaining wall to the finished soil level behind it. It is crucial for determining structural requirements and is different from the visible height above ground level.

When is a Form 15 and Form 16 required for retaining walls in Brisbane? Form 15 (Design Certificate) is typically required for engineered retaining walls, certifying the design meets relevant standards. Form 16 (Inspection Certificate) is needed for the inspection and compliance of the built work, confirming it matches the design.

Can I build a retaining wall without council approval? Brisbane City Council has specific circumstances where a retaining wall may not require formal building approval. However, factors like height, surcharge loads, boundary proximity, and easements can trigger approval requirements. It's essential to check with the council or consult the Queensland Development Code.
