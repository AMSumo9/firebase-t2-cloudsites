Two Brisbane builders can both look credible on paper while pricing completely different jobs. The published renovation guide keeps returning to the same fix: write the brief in job language before anyone quotes. Say whether the work is an internal refresh, a structural change inside the existing shell, or a build that changes the footprint. The categories matter because a room refit, an internal structural rework and a raise and build under project each carry different approval steps, consultant input and site programs, even when all three are casually called renovations.

The three pathways owners actually choose between

Most local decisions reduce to three pathways. Each one changes who you should shortlist and how the quotes should read.

  • Contained room work. Kitchens and bathrooms turn on layout, fixtures, finishes and services. The guide's timing cues are 4 to 8 weeks for kitchens and 2 to 4 weeks for bathrooms.
  • Structural work within the shell. Removing or moving load bearing elements raises the documentation, permit and sequencing load, and usually needs a building permit.
  • Footprint changing work. Extensions and raise and build under projects follow a longer pre start path, and council approval applies when the footprint or structure changes.

A builder strong on contemporary internal refits is not automatically the right fit for a project mixing character features with structural changes. Match the pathway first, then the shortlist.

Reading the cost ranges like a filter

The guide's figures are best treated as a way to rule options in or out, not as verdicts on what you should spend. Kitchens typically sit between $25,000 and $60,000+. Bathrooms commonly come in around $22,000 to $35,000+. Extensions generally start near $150,000, while full home projects begin at $150,000+ and frequently fall between $150,000 and $500,000+ depending on scope. A standard 3 to 4 bedroom full renovation is described around $250,000 to $450,000, and raise and build under work starts from $650,000.

Those numbers only do work when each quote states what is inside them. If one proposal includes demolition, documentation and coordination, and another leaves design detail or structural items open, the totals describe different jobs. Ask each builder to list inclusions, exclusions and any assumptions that could move the price later.

Approvals decide when work really starts

The timing guidance is more useful than any construction promise. The guide breaks a project into feasibility, design and documentation, pre construction, and construction, which is closer to how owners actually experience it. Kitchens often take 4 to 8 weeks, bathrooms 2 to 4 weeks, and full home renovations 5 to 12 months once approvals are included.

The approval triggers are straightforward. Council approval applies when a renovation alters the building footprint or structure. A building permit is needed for structural alterations, extensions and significant layout changes. Internal only renovations typically do not need planning approval. So the better question is not who can start first. Ask what must be resolved before a start date is reliable, including drawings, permits and any extra approval tied to the house type.

Older Brisbane homes raise the bar on the brief

The most useful local detail in the guide concerns Queenslander and heritage properties. It points to VJ walls, timber restoration and raise and build under projects, and notes that heritage overlay properties need additional approval. Those details change both builder fit and how a quote should be structured.

Ask each builder to split restoration items from new work. That separation shows whether the price reflects the existing fabric, the layout changes and the approval tasks, instead of hiding everything inside one broad allowance. For whole home projects, the guide describes a design build service with a single point of contact. If that appeals, make sure the role is written into the scope rather than left as a verbal summary.

Who this comparison helps most

This approach suits owners who know which rooms or spaces are involved but have not settled the pathway. It helps when you are deciding between a contained room update and a larger construction brief, when a single point of contact sounds attractive but must be defined in writing, and when the house type could change approvals or the timing of site work.

The guide notes an initial form taking under 2 minutes, with 2 to 3 quotes supplied within 48 hours. That speed is useful as a test: it shows quickly whether different builders are pricing the same brief, and whether the job belongs in a room based scope or a broader construction process.

  1. Write the brief in job language. Classify the work as an internal refresh, structural change within the shell, or a footprint changing build, and describe rooms and approvals plainly.
  2. Request quotes on the same brief. Send identical scope documents so each builder prices the same rooms, structural items and approval tasks.
  3. Compare inclusions, exclusions and assumptions. Check each quote line by line for demolition, documentation and coordination, and question any structural item left unresolved.
  4. Resolve approvals before accepting a start date. Confirm drawings, building permits and any heritage overlay approval are settled before relying on the program.
