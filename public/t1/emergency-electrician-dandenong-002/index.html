The first description helps distinguish a dead circuit from a wider supply problem. 111 Electrical asks customers to describe the electrical work, access conditions and anything nearby that could affect the job.

One room losing power is different from a whole street going dark. The business states that Dandenong is served by United Energy, so a street wide outage is usually a supply issue rather than a fault inside one property. A breaker that repeatedly trips or a scorched power point points to an issue requiring inspection.

For smoke, flames or electric shock, keep clear of anything live and call 000. Include which circuit is affected and whether the problem began when an appliance or other equipment was used.

Match the response to the electrical problem

A breaker that will not stay up may require fault finding. An older board with ceramic fuses may need modern breakers and safety switches. A dead or scorched outlet may need replacement, while tired wiring may require renewal beyond one fitting.

  • For an outage, state whether one circuit, one room or the whole property is affected.
  • For a switchboard concern, mention ceramic fuses, repeated tripping or equipment that trips the board under load.
  • For extra outlets or rewiring, identify the rooms, fittings and access conditions.
  • For a split system, identify the room because the system usually needs its own circuit and must suit the switchboard.

Electrical safety checks described by the business include testing wiring, earthing, switchboards and safety switches in homes and rentals, with hazards flagged.

Give different details for homes and businesses

In a home or rental, the fault may be isolated to one circuit. A shop, office, factory or warehouse may have three phase power and a larger board. State the property type, affected equipment and available access before the visit.

Residential requests may instead involve an emergency fault, switchboard upgrade, additional power point, rewiring or safety check. These details affect the questions an electrician needs to ask.

Prepare a useful quote request

Include the suburb or postcode, property type, affected room or circuit, what changed before the fault and whether the switchboard or outlet can be reached. For a business, name the equipment and say whether the site uses three phase power.

A useful example is a rental with one dead room and a breaker that trips whenever an appliance starts. Record the room, the breaker position, the appliance involved and whether other rooms still have power. That gives the electrician a clearer starting point than saying only that the house has stopped working.

Before booking, ask four practical questions: what part of the installation will be inspected, what information should be available on arrival, whether the agreed price covers the described scope, and what could require further approval. Also ask who will attend if an apprentice is mentioned.

Common questions

What should I do if a breaker keeps tripping? Describe which breaker is affected, what was running and whether it will reset. Repeated tripping may require fault finding or a switchboard assessment.

How much does an emergency electrician charge? The cost depends on the fault and is priced after the job is understood. The published $350 to $650 guide applies to common oven, cooktop or rangehood connections in October 2026, not every emergency.

Does electrical work come with paperwork? The published guidance says completed electrical installation work in Victoria comes with a Certificate of Electrical Safety. Keep it with the property records.

Can an apprentice work on the job? An apprentice may help, but a licensed electrician supervises regulated work and provides the certificate when the work is finished.

What is the difference between an electrician and a registered electrical contractor? The electrician is the licensed person doing the wiring. The registered electrical contractor is the business taking responsibility for the work, and one person can hold both roles.
