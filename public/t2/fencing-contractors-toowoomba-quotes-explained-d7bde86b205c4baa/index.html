A quote for a boundary fence, pool barrier or rural run can look complete while leaving out the items that decide cost and finish. A metre rate can help sanity-check a project, but it does not describe the site. The smarter reading starts with what the fence must do, then checks that every line item needed to deliver that outcome is written down.

Two quotes for the same postcode can cover different work. One may assume removal of the old fence, treatment of posts and raked panels for a slope; another may price a straight run on flat ground only. Until the inclusions are set side by side, the figures are not comparable.

The Line Items That Decide a Quote

When reviewing quotes, work through the items that most often change the job:

  • Removal. Whether the old fence, its posts and any old concrete are removed and disposed of.
  • Post treatment. How posts are set, treated and positioned, especially at corners and returns.
  • Gate hardware. Hinges, latches and any allowance for future automation at the driveway.
  • Slope treatment. Whether panels will be stepped or raked where the block falls, and how post positions change with it.
  • Hard ground handling. Any special provision for rock, old concrete or difficult access along a side boundary.
  • Repair limits. For repairs, which sections are being fixed and which are excluded from the price.

Rangeville and Mount Lofty blocks are useful examples of where slope can affect post positions, panel treatment and setting-out work, so a flat-rate assumption rarely holds there.

Comparing Quotes Without Guesswork

Alignment comes from describing the same job to each contractor. A short description is enough to open the quote conversation, and there is no obligation and no deposit at that stage. The description should cover the run, the suburb or postcode, whether an old fence must be removed, and whether the line touches a pool, retaining wall or neighbour boundary.

  • Put the fence's purpose in writing: privacy, pool safety, street presentation, security, rural use or repair.
  • Flag slope, retaining walls, corner sight lines, narrow side access and rear boundaries in the enquiry, not at the site visit.
  • Ask each contractor to state exclusions, not only inclusions.
  • Check the QBCC licence is current and covers the job's scope before weighing up colours, styles or extras.

Boundary Splits and What the Quote Must Record

A boundary fence can be both a building decision and a neighbour conversation. The Neighbourhood Disputes (Dividing Fences and Trees) Act 2011 sets the process for adjoining owners considering a sufficient dividing fence. A premium material, extra height or preferred finish is not automatically shared, which is why the written description of work matters: it should state the intended line, material, height, gates and any removal.

The practical sequence is to begin with at least one quote, discuss the proposal with the neighbour, and issue a notice to contribute if discussion does not settle the matter. There is a one month period to reach agreement before a possible application to QCAT. Where the planned height, a retaining wall, corner visibility or a pool barrier raises approval questions, record those facts in the quote brief so the line items reflect them.

Repair Quotes Versus Replacement Quotes

Repairs deserve the same discipline as new work. Leaning posts, broken rails and storm-damaged runs may be repaired, but straight replacement can be the better path where repair is not economical. That decision rests on the condition of the sound parts of the run, not only the most visible broken section.

A repair quote should name the sections to be fixed, the hardware to be reused or replaced, and the limit of the work. If the run is near end of life, a replacement quote with removal included gives a cleaner comparison than patching one section at a time.

Who This Applies To

This approach suits property owners comparing a new boundary fence, a replacement run, a gate, a pool barrier, a privacy screen or a repair after damage. For a family home the deciding questions are often privacy, children, pets and how a gate works with the driveway. For a character home, timber style and street presentation carry more weight. For acreage or lifestyle blocks, rural materials such as ringlock, strained wire, star pickets or post and rail belong in the brief. For a business premises or school, chain-wire, tubular steel and commercial-grade fencing point the conversation in a different direction, and the quote should be read against that use, not a domestic boundary brief.

  1. Describe the run. Give the suburb or postcode, the approximate fence line and whether an old fence is in the way.
  2. Name the purpose. State whether the priority is privacy, pool safety, street presentation, security, rural use or repair.
  3. Flag site constraints. Mention slope, retaining walls, corner visibility, hard ground, old concrete and narrow access.
  4. Check licence scope. For work requiring licensing, confirm a current QBCC licence with the right scope for the job.
  5. Compare written inclusions. Review materials, gates, removal, posts, corners, returns and repair limits before treating quotes as comparable.
