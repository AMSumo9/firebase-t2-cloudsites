Business Equipment Finance Costs Explained

The specific interest rate applied to business equipment finance is not universal. It is largely influenced by the risk profile of the business and the resale value of the asset being funded. Lenders assess whether the machinery, vehicle or technology holds its value over time. For example, industrial machinery and heavy construction equipment often attract different rates compared to office technology or medical devices. The business equipment finance structure selected also plays a role. A chattel mortgage might offer different tax deductions compared to a finance lease, which indirectly affects the net cost of borrowing. Because these variables shift based on market conditions and the specific lender, businesses usually work with a broker to identify the most competitive rate for their specific asset class.

Deposits and borrowing capacity

One of the primary costs to consider is the initial deposit or contribution required by the lender. Standard practice in the industry sees deposits range from 10 to 30 per cent of the equipment's purchase price. This contribution reduces the lender's risk and demonstrates the commitment of the borrower. However, this is not a fixed rule for all scenarios. Businesses with a strong trading history and solid financials may negotiate terms that require little to no deposit. In some cases, established businesses can secure up to 100 per cent financing. This means the full cost of the asset is covered by the loan, allowing the business to retain its cash reserves for wages, stock or unexpected expenses. The ability to borrow the full amount often depends on the age and type of equipment, as well as the credit history of the company.

Fees, charges and insurance

Aside from the principal and interest, several other costs contribute to the total expense of business equipment finance. Borrowers should anticipate potential establishment fees, documentation fees or application processing charges. These upfront costs vary between lenders and sometimes depend on the complexity of the deal. Additionally, if the finance arrangement includes a balloon payment at the end of the term, the business must plan for this lump sum. While a balloon lowers monthly repayments, it represents a significant cost at the conclusion of the contract. Lenders almost universally mandate that the funded asset be insured comprehensively for the duration of the loan. This insurance protects the financier's security interest and must be factored into the running costs. The premium for this insurance will vary based on the value of the machinery or vehicle and the business's claims history.

Repayment structures and terms

The length of the loan term directly impacts the total interest paid and the size of each repayment. Most business equipment finance terms are structured between one and seven years. Aligning the term with the useful life of the asset is a common strategy; this ensures the equipment is generating revenue while it is being paid off. Shorter terms mean higher monthly repayments but less total interest paid over the life of the loan. Conversely, longer terms reduce the monthly strain on cash flow but increase the total cost due to the accumulation of interest. Businesses must also decide between principal and interest repayments or interest-only periods, if available. Common questions

What are the main costs involved in equipment finance? The main costs include the interest payments on the borrowed amount, any upfront establishment or application fees, and ongoing charges. Borrowers must also account for insurance costs on the asset and potentially a balloon payment if the loan structure includes one.

Can I finance the entire cost of the equipment? Yes, it is possible to finance the entire cost. While standard deposits are often between 10 and 30 per cent, established businesses with strong trading histories can often secure up to 100 per cent financing from certain lenders.

How does the loan term affect the total cost? The loan term dictates how long you pay interest. Shorter terms generally mean higher monthly repayments but lower total interest costs. Longer terms ease monthly cash flow but result in paying more interest over the life of the facility.
