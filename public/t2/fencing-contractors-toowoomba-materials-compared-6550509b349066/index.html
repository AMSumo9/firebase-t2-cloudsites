A useful comparison begins with what the fence must achieve on the block. Privacy, airflow, street presentation, pool compliance, rural containment and commercial security point to different materials, heights and gate details. Measure the approximate length, corners, returns and direction changes before choosing a finish. Note pedestrian gates, driveway gates or automation, plus access limits, established landscaping and old fencing that may affect inspection. Flag whether the run adjoins a pool, retaining wall, corner block or neighbour boundary, because those features can change layout, compliance and price.

  • Define the line first: boundary, internal division or replacement of an existing run.
  • State the purpose in plain terms: privacy, pool barrier, rural line, security screen or repair.
  • Record constraints: slope, tight access, retaining walls, corner sight lines and removal.
  • Keep personal upgrades separate from the shared standard when a neighbour may contribute.

Boundary and neighbour checks

A dividing fence can be both construction work and a neighbour process. The guide refers to Queensland's Neighbourhood Disputes (Dividing Fences and Trees) Act 2011 for adjoining owners considering a sufficient dividing fence. It also notes that a premium material, extra height or preferred finish is not automatically shared. Establish the intended line and proposed standard before comparing prices. A written description and at least one quote give both owners something specific to review. If agreement is not reached, the formal step is described as a notice to contribute, with one month to reach agreement before a possible QCAT application within the stated period. Height needs care where ground level, retaining walls or corner visibility are involved; combined height can change the approval question, and a corner block can need a lower sight-line treatment so drivers can see across the intersection.

Read the quote inclusions

A metre rate is only a rough check. Two prices are not comparable unless they treat posts, corners, gate hardware, existing fence condition, access and sloping sections in the same way. Rangeville and Mount Lofty are named as examples where slope can require stepped panels or a raked line; that does not make every property there difficult, but it shows why the quote should respond to the actual run. Ask for material, height, posts, gate approach and repair scope to be visible in writing. Where licensing is required, check that the contractor holds a current QBCC licence with the right scope for the job. For repairs, ask whether leaning posts, broken rails or storm-damaged sections can be fixed, or whether replacement is more economical, based on the condition of the run and the written repair scope.

Who this applies to

This angle suits Toowoomba property owners planning a boundary fence, gate, pool barrier, privacy screen, rural line, commercial edge or repair. It is most useful before a material is locked in, or when quotes seem to describe different work. It also applies where a neighbour may be asked to contribute. Keep the shared boundary question separate from personal choices such as a preferred finish, added screening or extra height, so the quote conversation stays tied to the proposed fence rather than assumptions about payment for every feature.

  1. Frame the run. Write down the boundary or internal line, approximate length, corners, returns, access limits and any old fence to remove.
  2. Name the job. Choose the main task: privacy, pool safety, rural containment, security, street presentation or repair.
  3. Shortlist materials. Match Colorbond, timber, aluminium, PVC, pool or rural types to that task rather than appearance alone.
  4. Check shared boundary issues. Note neighbour contribution, extra height, premium finish and any notice step before comparing prices.
  5. Compare written inclusions. Read posts, corners, gates, slope treatment, repair scope and QBCC licence scope where required.
