Conveyancing Sydney: What To Check Before Exchange

Last updated: July 2026

For buyers and sellers comparing conveyancing sydney options, the useful question is not just who will handle the file, but what is included before exchange, what remains extra, and how settlement will be managed.

Conveyancing Sydney Explained

When you review Sydney Conveyancers, the most useful signal is the written split between the professional fee and the extra costs that sit outside it. The source page says routine matters are commonly quoted on a fixed-fee basis, while government charges, searches and transfer duty are set out separately before you engage anyone.

That distinction matters because two quotes can look similar until one includes only the legal work and the other lists expected outlays line by line. Before you sign, ask for the exact scope of contract review, searches, exchange support, settlement attendance through PEXA, and any added work that could move the matter outside a routine file.

  • Check whether the quote is for a purchase, sale, transfer, off-the-plan matter or commercial property.
  • Confirm that disbursements are itemised in writing rather than rolled into a vague estimate.
  • Ask who will handle questions if the contract reveals an issue before exchange.

Which professional suits the transaction

The source page makes a clear NSW distinction. A property matter may be handled by a licensed conveyancer, regulated by NSW Fair Trading under the Conveyancers Licensing Act 2003, or by a solicitor. It also notes that a licensed conveyancer specialises in property transfers, while a solicitor can advise on related legal issues.

For a straightforward purchase or sale, the practical comparison is experience with the exact transaction type. First home buyers may need someone who can explain each step to settlement. A family title transfer needs careful document handling. Commercial matters can involve leases, GST and broader due diligence. Off-the-plan matters raise contract timing and disclosure questions that should be checked well before completion.

If you are choosing between providers, ask less about titles and more about recent work on the same type of file, who will review the contract, and when complex issues are escalated.

Decision points that change the risk profile

Not every property file follows the same timetable. The source page specifically highlights first home buyer, off-the-plan, auction and commercial matters, and each one changes what should happen before you commit.

For auction purchases, the major issue is timing. The page states there is no cooling-off period at auction in NSW, so the contract needs review before you bid rather than after. For private treaty purchases, the same source says most residential contracts in NSW have a 5 business day cooling-off period from exchange of contracts, ending at 5pm on the fifth business day.

Off-the-plan buyers should focus on the contract terms and disclosure rules that apply when buying before construction is complete. Sellers should check that the contract for sale and prescribed disclosure documents are prepared correctly before the property is marketed. Commercial buyers and sellers should test whether the adviser is also checking lease terms, GST treatment and settlement requirements, not only transfer paperwork.

What happens between contract review and settlement

The page sets out a practical file path: review the contract, run the searches, calculate transfer duty and manage settlement through PEXA. That gives you a simple checklist for comparing services, because each stage affects timing and cost visibility.

Contract review is where unusual clauses, disclosure gaps or settlement dates are identified early. Searches help confirm what is being transferred and whether further questions are needed. Transfer duty should be calculated before commitment so there is no confusion about funds required. PEXA settlement adds a more transparent digital process for document lodgement and transfer of ownership, which the page describes as faster and clearer.

If a quote feels thin, ask whether the written scope covers pre-exchange advice, communication with the other side, preparation for settlement day and post-settlement confirmation. Those details often show the difference between a low headline fee and a well-scoped matter.

Who this guide applies to

This guide is most useful for Sydney buyers and sellers who want a clear written scope before engaging a conveyancer or solicitor. It also fits readers comparing a first home purchase, an investment property sale, an off-the-plan contract, an auction bid, a family title transfer or a commercial property matter.

If your file is routine, the immediate goal is a fixed professional fee confirmed in writing with disbursements listed separately. If your file is less standard, the better question is what extra legal work could arise and whether the adviser you choose handles those issues personally.

  • Buyers should bring the draft contract, planned signing date and any auction deadline.
  • Sellers should ask when the contract for sale and disclosure documents will be ready.
  • Families transferring title should ask what documents and identity checks will be needed.
  • Commercial parties should ask how leases, GST and due diligence affect the scope.

The source page also offers a cost calculator and a settlement checklist, which are useful prompts when you want to compare two written quotes on the same assumptions.

  1. Identify the file type. State whether the matter is a purchase, sale, transfer, auction, off-the-plan or commercial transaction before comparing quotes.
  2. Request the written fee split. Ask for the fixed professional fee and the separate list of disbursements, government charges and transfer duty assumptions in writing.
  3. Test the pre-exchange scope. Confirm who reviews the contract, runs the searches and answers issues before you commit to the transaction.
  4. Check settlement method and timing. Ask how PEXA settlement will be managed and what milestones apply from contract review through to settlement day.

Common questions

How much does conveyancing usually cost in Sydney? The source page says routine professional fees are typically a few hundred to around $1,500, and that many conveyancers offer a fixed-fee quote. It also states that government charges and disbursements such as title searches and registration are extra and vary by property.

Do I need a licensed conveyancer or a solicitor in NSW? Either can handle the matter. The source page says a licensed conveyancer is regulated by NSW Fair Trading under the Conveyancers Licensing Act 2003, while a solicitor can also advise on related legal issues.

Why is auction buying treated differently? Because the source page states there is no cooling-off period at auction in NSW. That is why contract review needs to happen before you bid, not after you are successful.

What steps should be included in the service? The source page points to contract review, searches, transfer duty calculation and settlement through PEXA. For sellers, it also refers to preparing the contract for sale and attaching the prescribed disclosure documents.
