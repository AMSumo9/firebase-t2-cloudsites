Private Lending Australia Explained

Bottom Line Finance describes private lending as commercial, business-purpose capital from non-bank and private lenders, secured against property or other assets. That wording is important because it separates this type of funding from consumer credit, owner-occupied home loans and general personal borrowing.

The published position is deal-led: the borrower sends the project, numbers and timeline, then the structure is tested against the security and the desired outcome. A developer settling a site, refinancing completed dwellings, funding construction draws or filling a capital stack gap needs a different conversation from a household comparing standard home loan repayments.

A sensible first review should identify the borrower, the asset, the purpose, the repayment path and the reason a bank process may not suit. If those basics are unclear, the quote can look precise while the structure is still weak.

Who this applies to

This guide applies to commercial borrowers, developers and asset-backed applicants considering non-bank or private lender funding. The source material names residential, commercial, industrial, specialised assets, childcare, farm and agribusiness as areas where funding may be arranged when the deal and security stack up.

That breadth does not mean every property or borrower type will qualify. The page states that outcomes vary by deal and that case studies are illustrative only. The practical borrower question is therefore: what part of the transaction needs capital, and what asset or cash flow supports repayment?

  • Developers may need land, build or cost-to-completion funding.
  • Asset-backed businesses may need capital where a standard bank box is too narrow.
  • Investors or owners with completed stock may need to release equity from finished but unsold dwellings.
  • Rural or specialised asset borrowers may need lenders who understand the operator and the income profile.

Choosing the right structure

The service list points to different problems rather than interchangeable products. Construction finance is described as funding a build in progress with draws released as work is completed. Residual stock finance is for unsold, completed dwellings after practical completion. Bridging finance is short-term capital for a timing gap.

Mezzanine finance and preferred equity sit in a different part of the decision. The source page describes mezzanine finance as a subordinated layer behind the senior lender and ahead of equity. That makes capital stack position, repayment priority and control of the next deal more relevant than a simple rate comparison.

Borrowers wanting a broader primer can compare the related private lending guide with this page, then return to the project numbers. The useful documents are usually the project summary, security details, amount required, existing debt position and timeline.

Questions to ask before requesting terms

Private funding conversations move faster when the borrower asks questions that match the risk. ASIC Moneysmart explains that home loan borrowers should compare loan features, fees and repayment ability. For commercial property funding, the same discipline becomes a project-level check rather than a household budget exercise.

  • What is the exact purpose of funds, acquisition, construction, refinance, residual stock, bridging or capital stack support?
  • What security is being offered, and is the asset residential, commercial, industrial, specialised, childcare or rural?
  • What event repays the facility, sale, refinance, settlement, completion or another source?
  • Where does the proposed debt sit relative to senior debt and equity?
  • Which costs affect the return on equity, not just the headline rate?
  • What happens if the sale, build, refinance or settlement date moves?

Those questions keep the assessment tied to the published themes of equity protection, return on equity, timing, risk and future deal capacity.

Australia-wide context without suburb stuffing

The source page lists Sydney, Melbourne, Brisbane, Perth, Adelaide, the Gold Coast, Sunshine Coast and regional NSW, QLD, VIC and WA. Those places matter because development funding is shaped by asset type, deal timing and exit path, not because a location name alone proves suitability.

A regional agribusiness security needs different lender understanding from an apartment tower, a childcare project, an industrial asset or a short-stay commercial property. The published text also notes that specialised assets and social infrastructure need lenders who understand income, the operator and long-term value.

For private lending australia research, treat location as one input in the capital structure. Ask how the lender views the asset class, completion risk, tenant or operator income, existing debt and repayment event. Avoid relying on generic claims about grants, concessions, postcode rules or market trends unless they are separately sourced for the actual transaction.

  1. Define the transaction. Write the funding purpose, project location, amount required, existing debt and intended repayment event in one clear summary.
  2. Map the security. Identify the property or asset backing the facility and whether it is residential, commercial, industrial, specialised, childcare, rural or agribusiness.
  3. Match the product to the problem. Use the service menu to separate bridging, construction, residual stock, mezzanine and development needs before comparing terms.
  4. Test value beyond rate. Compare the cost of capital against equity protection, timing risk, return on equity and the ability to complete the next deal.

Common questions

Is this the same as a home loan? No. The source page states that the provider arranges commercial and business-purpose funding only and does not arrange consumer credit or owner-occupied home loans.

What assets are mentioned in the published material? The page refers to residential, commercial, industrial, specialised assets, childcare, farm and agribusiness funding where the deal and security stack up.

Does the cheapest rate always mean the best outcome? The source material says the cheapest rate is rarely the best outcome and frames value around return on equity, time, risk and future deal capacity.

Can terms be assumed from past examples? No. The page states that case studies are illustrative of past transactions, outcomes vary by deal and they do not represent current or typical terms.
