What belongs on the scope list

A kitchen renovations eastern melbourne earns its keep by forcing every job, big or small, to be written down. The full scope can include demolition and strip-out, rough-in plumbing and electrical, cabinetry and joinery, benchtop templating and installation, splashback, appliances, finishing, and the handover paperwork.

  • Demolition and strip-out, plus how hidden damage will be documented and priced if found
  • Rough-in work where plumbing or electrical positions change
  • Cabinetry, including whether it is rebuilt or refaced
  • Benchtops and splashback, including edge detail and fabrication limits
  • Appliances, lighting, ventilation and compliance items
  • Finishing trades and the final inspection or handover documents

Each project starts with an in-home measure-up and an itemised fixed-scope quote. Test assumptions at that point: what is included, what is excluded, how surprises after strip-out are handled, and how variations are recorded.

Timelines, contracts and the paperwork loop

The renovation sequence runs measure-up, then design and quote, then build and install, then handover. For a full rebuild, allow about 6 to 12 weeks from signing to handover. Cabinetry and stone fabrication take roughly 4 to 6 weeks of lead time, followed by usually 2 to 4 weeks on site covering installation, plumbing, electrical, tiling and finishing.

Under the Domestic Building Contracts Act 1995, a written contract is required for a major domestic building contract over $10,000. Domestic building insurance is required on residential work above the current threshold, commonly cited at $16,000, and the live figure should be confirmed with the Victorian Building Authority.

Ask for scope, price, dates, payment stages, the variation process, warranty terms and compliance paperwork in writing. A fixed-price quote is only useful when the scope behind it is fixed enough to compare like with like.

Benchtop choices after the silica ban

The benchtop conversation changed when engineered stone with 1% or more crystalline silica was banned for manufacture, supply, processing and installation across Australia from 1 July 2024, under the model WHS Regulations administered by Safe Work Australia.

The workable alternatives now include porcelain and sintered stone, natural granite or marble, solid timber, stainless steel, laminate, and engineered stone under the 1% crystalline silica threshold. Choose by use rather than colour alone: heat, staining, chopping habits, edge profile, seam visibility, sink and tap detail, weight, cleaning and repair all matter once the kitchen is in daily service.
