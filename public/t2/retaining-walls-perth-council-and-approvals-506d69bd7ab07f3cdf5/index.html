Retaining Walls Perth Council and Approvals Explained

Homeowners in Perth must understand that a local government building permit is generally required once a retaining wall retains more than 0.5 metres of ground. This height threshold is a primary trigger for council assessment. However, the source text notes that a wall of any height can need approval if it is tied to other building work, affects or protects adjoining land, or impacts a boundary wall or dividing fence. This means a low garden wall might still require a permit if it sits close to a neighbour's property line or supports a driveway.

The distinction between a garden terrace and a structural retaining wall is crucial for determining the approval pathway. A wall that merely holds back soil for a garden bed might fall under different regulations than a wall supporting a driveway or a building structure. The source text emphasises that the function of the wall is a key factor in deciding whether a permit is necessary. Owners should not assume that a wall below the 0.5 metre mark is automatically exempt from council oversight, particularly if it interacts with neighbouring properties or existing structures.

Another critical trigger is the presence of surcharge. If the wall supports a driveway, a building, or a fence, the load on the wall increases. This added load can change the classification of the wall from a simple garden feature to a structural element that requires engineering and approval. The source text advises owners to document the wall's function and the loads it carries before approaching the council to ensure the correct permit is sought.

Tiered walls and council rules

Tiered retaining walls present a unique challenge for approval because the combined height of the structure can exceed the standard 0.5 metre trigger. The source text highlights that some local councils, including Wanneroo and Melville, count the combined height of tiered retaining toward the 0.5 metre figure. This means that even if each individual tier is below the threshold, the total height might require a permit. Owners should verify the specific rules of their local government, as council requirements can vary significantly across Perth.

When dealing with tiered walls, the source text suggests that owners confirm the actual requirement with their local government before construction starts. This proactive approach prevents costly delays or the need to demolish and rebuild a wall that was not approved. The complexity of tiered walls often necessitates a more detailed application, including drawings that show the relationship between the different levels and the drainage requirements for each tier.

The visual impact of tiered walls on the streetscape is also a consideration. Some councils may have specific design guidelines for retaining walls that are visible from the front of the property. These guidelines might dictate the materials, face finish, or height limits for walls that are part of the front boundary. Owners should review these guidelines to ensure their design complies with both the structural and aesthetic requirements of the council.

Engineering and design standards

For walls that require a permit, compliance with engineering standards is mandatory. The source text names AS 4678 as the relevant standard for retaining walls. This Australian standard provides the technical requirements for the design, construction, and safety of retaining walls. A wall that meets the 0.5 metre height threshold or is located near a boundary will typically need to be designed by a WA registered building engineering practitioner.

The role of the engineering practitioner is to provide design drawings and certification that demonstrate the wall's stability and safety. This certification is a key requirement for the council approval process. The source text advises owners to ask who is responsible for the design drawings, engineering certification, and permit documents. In many cases, the builder or contractor will engage the engineer on behalf of the owner, but it is important to clarify this responsibility before work begins.

Engineering also covers the drainage requirements of the wall. The source text treats drainage as part of the structure, not an optional extra. An engineer will specify the type and placement of drainage materials, such as ag drain, gravel, and geofabric, to prevent water from building up behind the wall. This is particularly important in Perth's sandy soils, which can become saturated during winter. A wall that is properly engineered will include a comprehensive drainage system to ensure its long-term performance.

What to include in the application

When applying for a permit, owners must provide detailed documentation to the council. This typically includes site plans that show the location of the wall, its height, and its relationship to the property boundaries. The source text notes that a practical brief should ask who is responsible for the design drawings, engineering certification, permit documents, and any inspections required by the approval pathway.

The application process may also require inspections at various stages of construction. The council may want to inspect the excavation, the placement of the drainage, and the pouring of concrete. Owners should ensure that their builder or contractor is aware of these inspection requirements and can schedule them accordingly. Failure to arrange inspections can result in the council issuing a notice to rectify or stop work.

Owners should also consider the impact of the wall on neighbouring properties. If the wall is close to a fence or a neighbour's building, the council may require a report from a surveyor or an engineer to assess the risk of damage. This is especially important for walls that are retaining soil that is higher than the neighbour's ground level. The source text advises owners to document the wall's proximity to fences, boundaries, or paved areas when applying for a permit.

Common questions

Do I need a permit for a 0.4m retaining wall? A permit is generally not required for a wall under 0.5 metres high unless it affects adjoining land, is tied to other building work, or impacts a boundary wall or dividing fence.

What is AS 4678? AS 4678 is the Australian standard for retaining walls. It provides the technical requirements for the design, construction, and safety of retaining structures.

Which councils count tiered wall heights? Some councils, including Wanneroo and Melville, count the combined height of tiered retaining structures toward the 0.5 metre threshold for permit requirements.
