Key takeaway

Commercial construction loans operate on a progress draw basis, meaning interest accrues only on the funds that have been drawn down rather than the total loan limit. Pricing is driven by the loan to cost and loan to gross realisable value ratios, with interest commonly capitalised during the build phase to assist cash flow. While non-bank lenders may charge higher rates, they often provide higher leverage and require less presale cover compared to major banks.

Construction Loans Costs Explained

Unlike standard mortgages where the principal is available immediately, construction loans fund the build through a series of progress draws. This structure significantly impacts the overall cost because interest generally accrues only on the drawn balance, not the total facility limit. As each build stage is completed and verified by a quantity surveyor, the lender releases the next tranche of funds. This approach ensures the borrower is not paying interest on money that is not yet being used for construction, creating interest efficiency over the life of the project.

The process involves a retention amount held back by the lender until practical completion is reached. While this protects the lender's security, it requires the developer to manage cash flow carefully during the final stages to ensure the builder can be paid in full without delays. The cost of engaging a quantity surveyor to verify these stages is an essential part of the financing expense, ensuring the drawdowns align strictly with the value of work completed.

Interest capitalisation and variable pricing

One of the most distinct features of construction finance is the treatment of interest. Rather than making monthly interest payments out of pocket, the interest is commonly capitalised within the facility throughout the construction period. This means the interest charges are added to the loan balance and settled at completion. While this improves cash flow during the build, vital for developers managing multiple outgoings, it does increase the total loan amount and the eventual repayment burden.

Pricing is not a one-size-fits-all figure. It is determined by several variables including the loan to cost, the loan to value against the end value, and the strength of the builder. Projects with strong presale cover and lower leverage typically attract keener pricing. Conversely, a higher-leverage structure with lower presales carries a higher cost of capital. The decision to use a more expensive facility often comes down to return on equity: if the higher cost allows a developer to preserve equity and complete a profitable project, the math may justify the expense.

Loan sizing metrics and valuation fees

Lenders size facilities using dual gearing metrics rather than a single snapshot. They assess the loan to total development cost and the loan to value against the gross realisable value. This dual view provides a clearer picture of feasibility than a simple loan-to-value ratio. Establishing these metrics requires upfront costs, including detailed valuations and feasibility reports. Developers must budget for these professional fees as part of the initial entry cost.

A facility that tracks both TDC and GRV allows for a more nuanced approach to risk. For instance, a project might have a high cost but a significantly higher end value, supporting a larger loan amount. Understanding these calculations is critical for developers when budgeting for the costs associated with drawing down capital and ensuring the project remains solvent at every stage.

Bank versus non-bank cost structures

Major banks typically apply conservative policies regarding presale requirements and leverage caps. They offer lower interest rates but impose strict hurdles that can delay a project or require significant equity. When a project falls outside these parameters, such as needing higher leverage or having a tight settlement timeline, a non-bank lender becomes the viable option.

Non-bank lenders assess feasibility more holistically and can consider reduced or nil presale cover. This flexibility comes at a price, with higher interest rates reflecting the increased risk tolerance. However, for developers focused on opportunity cost, the speed and certainty of a non-bank facility can outweigh the premium paid on interest. The ability to proceed without selling half the project off the plan can be the difference between a stalled site and a completed development.

Exit strategies and residual considerations

The cost of a construction loan is heavily influenced by the clarity of the exit strategy. Lenders need to know how the facility will be repaid, whether through presales, a refinance, or a bulk sale. A clear exit reduces risk and can lead to better pricing. If the exit involves holding the asset, developers may look to refinance into a residual stock loan once practical completion is reached.

Residual stock loans release equity tied up in unsold, completed dwellings. This type of financing allows developers to pay out the construction facility and settle any remaining debts. Understanding the transition from construction finance to residual or long-term holding finance is crucial for calculating the total cost of capital over the entire project lifecycle, from land acquisition through to final stabilisation.

  1. Prepare feasibility studies. Calculate your Total Development Cost and Gross Realisable Value to understand your dual gearing metrics.
  2. Secure your exit. Establish a clear repayment strategy, whether through presales, refinance or residual stock loans.
  3. Engage professionals. Appoint a builder and quantity surveyor to provide the reports required for drawdown verification.
  4. Manage drawdowns. Submit progress claims as build stages are completed to ensure funds are released efficiently.

Common questions

How is interest calculated on a construction loan? Interest is typically charged only on the amount of money that has been drawn down, not the total loan limit. This interest is often capitalised, meaning it is added to the loan balance rather than paid monthly during the build.

What metrics do lenders use to size the loan? Lenders primarily use two metrics: Loan to Cost against the total development cost and Loan to Value against the gross realisable value. This dual metric approach provides a clearer view of project feasibility.

Can I get a construction loan without presales? Major banks usually require significant presale cover, but non-bank lenders can consider projects with reduced or nil presales if the feasibility and exit strategy are strong.
