Bathroom Renovations Melbourne Quotes Explained Explained

A useful quote reads like a recipe rather than a headline. It should state the room type, the demolition scope, the extent of waterproofing and tiling, the plumbing and electrical work, the fit-off, and which fixtures, tapware, cabinetry and stone are priced as firm selections instead of rough allowances. Labour is often a large share of the total where waterproofing, plumbing or electrical work is involved, so those lines deserve the closest reading.

When every stage is blended into one clean figure, the number explains very little. A written scope that lists each stage separately shows what is actually being bought and leaves less room for the brief to be reinterpreted once work begins.

  • Demolition and strip-out of the existing room
  • Waterproofing and substrate preparation
  • Plumbing and drainage, including any relocation
  • Electrical work, lighting and ventilation
  • Tiling and the final fit-off
  • Fixtures, tapware, cabinetry and stone, marked as fixed or allowance

Why two quotes for the same room can be thousands apart

A price gap between quotes is not automatically a red flag or a bargain. One contractor may assume the plumbing stays put while another prices a relocated shower and vanity, which widens both plumbing and waterproofing scope. One may include demolition, tiling and fit-off while another treats major selections as allowances to be priced later.

Part of the gap is genuine scope difference and part is assumption difference. That distinction sits at the centre of bathroom renovations melbourne myths, because a surprisingly low number is often a narrower job rather than a cheaper one.

Housing type sharpens the issue. An apartment with strata approval steps and owners-corporation access limits is a different quoting exercise from a period terrace with tight stairs or a family bathroom that needs durable, hard-wearing finishes. The tile brief can look identical across all three while the real work behind the walls is not.

Allowances, exclusions and the fine print

Australian renovation guidance from ASIC Moneysmart encourages homeowners to treat quotes and contracts as documents to interrogate, not sign. Two phrases do most of the hidden work in a bathroom quote. An allowance is a placeholder dollar figure for a selection not yet made, such as tapware or stone. A provisional sum covers work whose extent cannot be known until demolition, such as repairs to a damaged substrate.

Neither is dishonest, but both shift risk. If the final tile choice costs more than the allowance, the price rises. If rot, failed waterproofing or restricted access appears after strip-out, the provisional sum is consumed and then some. The fair question is not whether these items exist but how each contractor has estimated them and what happens when reality disagrees.

Exclusions matter just as much. Rubbish removal, temporary facilities, protection of adjoining floors, and making good after rough-in work are all lines that can quietly migrate between quotes.

Questions that expose quote gaps before you commit

Asking every contractor the same short set of questions turns a pile of unlike numbers into something comparable. The goal is to force assumptions onto paper, where they can be challenged.

  • Are demolition, waterproofing, tiling, plumbing, electrical and fit-off all included in this figure?
  • Which fixtures, tiles, tapware, cabinetry and stone are fixed selections, and which are allowances?
  • What happens if hidden waterproofing, access or substrate problems appear once work starts?
  • Is the price for a complete renovation, a compact layout improvement or a narrower repair?
  • What is expressly excluded, and what approvals or strata steps sit with the homeowner?

A quote that answers these clearly is usually more valuable than one that is simply lower, a point reinforced in common bathroom renovation myths for Melbourne homeowners.

The one-page brief that gets a sharper quote

The strongest lever a homeowner holds arrives before any quote does: a plain-language brief. It needs the suburb or postcode, the bathroom type, a rough scope, the current frustrations, any planned layout changes and every access or apartment complication. That is enough context for a contractor to judge whether the next step is a full quote, a cost review, an approvals conversation or a tighter layout rethink.

  1. Describe the room as it functions today, including leaks, ventilation, storage and clearance problems.
  2. State whether plumbing is expected to stay in place or move, since that single decision reshapes trade scope.
  3. Record access limits and body corporate rules before fixed pricing is requested.
  4. Separate essential repairs from finish preferences so the scope does not drift mid-negotiation.

End with one discipline: compare every quote against the same written scope. If one price assumes moving the shower and another assumes keeping it, the decision is not yet like for like.

  1. Map the room. Record the room type, dimensions, access constraints and whether plumbing is likely to stay in place.
  2. Split essentials from finishes. List leaks, ventilation, storage and safety needs separately from tiles, tapware, vanity and colour preferences.
  3. Request itemised inclusions. Ask for demolition, waterproofing, tiling, plumbing, electrical and fit-off as separate lines with fixed or allowance selections marked.
  4. Compare like with like. Match each quote against the same written scope, inclusions, exclusions and allowances before judging value.

Common questions

Why do bathroom renovation quotes in Melbourne vary so much? The typical full renovation range runs from about $8,000 to $35,000, and premium custom work can exceed $35,000. Most of the variation between quotes comes from scope and assumptions: layout changes, waterproofing extent, access limits and whether fixtures are fixed selections or allowances. Comparing totals without comparing those elements leads to false conclusions.

Is a quote near the bottom of the range a bad sign? Not automatically, but it needs interrogation. A low figure may reflect a compact scope, unchanged plumbing and modest finishes, or it may leave demolition, waterproofing or allowances open. Ask what is included, what is excluded and which assumptions could change before treating it as cheaper.

What should happen when hidden damage appears mid-job? A well-written scope states how concealed waterproofing, access or substrate problems are handled after work begins, including how variations are priced and approved. Homeowners should raise this scenario during quoting, not after demolition, and Australian renovating guidance from ASIC Moneysmart supports reviewing contract terms closely before signing.
