A first repair enquiry goes further when the wall has already been described on paper. From a few steps back, write down the material, the approximate height, any lean, bulge, crack or section that has sunk, and where damp appears after rain. Add what the retained side carries, such as a garden bed, level lawn, driveway edge, fence line or a change in boundary level. Note side access and gate clearances too, since machinery and spoil removal depend on them.

  • Photograph the face, the top of the wall and the drainage path, but keep the written description as the main record.
  • List anything above the retained area, including sheds, paved areas and neighbouring buildings.
  • Avoid guessing the cause in writing; record only what can be seen.

How water and weight steer the repair decision

Two forces do most of the damage to a retaining wall: ground pushing from behind and water that has nowhere to go. For that reason drainage belongs inside the scope of works, not at the end as a finishing touch. Ask how subsoil drainage, surface runoff and the discharge point will be handled, particularly where water ponds after rain or runs toward a neighbouring property. Recording where water collects and which way the ground falls turns a vague complaint into a testable question for the first inspection.

Weight is the second test. A low garden wall is a different job from one carrying a driveway, building, fence or a second wall. Where extra load, called surcharge, sits above the wall, the sensible question is whether an engineered or certifier-led path, or full replacement planning, is more appropriate than patching the face.

Approval triggers to raise before work starts

Building approval questions come up when retained fill rises more than 1 metre above natural ground, when the wall stands within 1.5 metres of a building or another retaining wall, or when surcharge loads such as a driveway, batter or building sit above it. A fence built on top can also invite siting discretion where the combined height passes 2 metres above natural ground, especially close to a boundary.

Treat those figures as questions for the provider, certifier or council, not guarantees. Natural ground level, boundary position, existing structures and the load above can each shift the answer. For boundary walls, council does not settle private responsibility; that turns on the land, the benefit it gives, prior works and legal facts, so contested cases may need neighbour discussion plus legal or dispute-resolution advice. Bodies such as the Housing Industry Association and Master Builders Australia publish general industry context that can help owners frame questions.

What belongs in a repair brief

A workable brief names the suburb or postcode, wall height, any material preference, timing, side access, drainage notes and what sits above the wall, covering fences, driveways, sheds, paved areas and nearby buildings. Keep photos ready but treat them as support for the written description, never a substitute.

Then ask each provider to state plainly whether the likely path is repair, replacement, new construction or design-led work, and whether engineering or certification is included. Materials come last, not first

Material choice follows diagnosis. Concrete sleepers suit sloping yards, boundary levelling and low-maintenance landscaping. Block walls pair with raised gardens, boundary changes and a stronger architectural finish. Timber sleepers fit lower garden beds, side paths and budget-conscious yard shaping. Locking in a material before the fault is understood risks paying for the wrong fix, so let the written scope settle the method before the finish is chosen.

  1. Record the wall condition. Write down material, approximate height, lean, cracking, bulging, sinking, damp signs and anything above the retained area.
  2. Map access and water. Note side access, spoil removal constraints, fences, driveways, surface runoff and where discharge will go.
  3. Check approval triggers. Compare the site against the 1 metre fill, 1.5 metre proximity and surcharge triggers, plus the 2 metre combined fence and wall question.
  4. Build the written brief. Combine condition notes, access limits, drainage observations and timing into one document to send to providers.
  5. Compare scoped quotes. Ask each provider to state whether the path is repair, replacement, new construction or design-led work, and what is included.

Common questions

Does a small crack mean the whole wall must be replaced? Not necessarily. The visible fault is read together with height, load, access and nearby structures. The useful step is to have a provider state whether the path is repair, replacement, new construction or design-led work, backed by a written scope of works.

Who pays when a boundary wall fails? Council does not decide private responsibility for boundary walls. The answer can depend on the land, the benefit the wall provides, prior works and legal facts, so disputed cases may need neighbour discussion and legal or dispute-resolution advice.

What should a written scope of works cover? It should identify what is being removed or retained, how drainage is treated, what access is assumed, whether temporary support is needed, and whether engineering or certification is included or excluded.
