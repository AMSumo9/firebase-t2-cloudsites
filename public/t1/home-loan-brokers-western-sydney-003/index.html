Deposit, LMI and borrowing power are the early filters

The source page gives a clear first screen. It says most lenders require at least a 5% genuine savings deposit. It also says Lenders Mortgage Insurance usually applies when you borrow more than 80% of the property value, and that repayments are tested at the actual rate plus the APRA 3% prudential buffer. Those checks narrow the field before a rate comparison means much.

For a first home buyer, that turns into a practical sequence. Is the deposit strong enough for the property being considered? If not, is the pressure point cash saved, borrowing capacity, or both? If borrowing is above 80% of value, what does that mean for LMI and the final loan size? The source page also mentions the First Home Guarantee and the NSW First Home Buyers Assistance Scheme, so a sensible next question is whether either is relevant to the purchase and whether the current eligibility settings still line up with the lender being considered.

How to test a broker review properly

A useful review explains assumptions, not just a rate. The source page says borrowers can compare loans and features from a wide panel of lenders, and it also refers to a licensed broker review as the next step. That gives you a practical standard to test. If the review cannot show what inputs were used, or why one structure is being preferred over another, it is too thin to guide a real application.

Ask what income, debts and living expenses were used for the borrowing assessment. Ask how fixed versus variable options, offset accounts, equity release and LMI have been considered for your case. If you are self-employed, ask whether full-doc or alt-doc pathways are relevant, because the source page presents both for business owners and contractors with variable income. If the matter is complex, ask whether any fee applies and have that disclosed in writing up front. For most standard home loans, the same source says the broker is usually paid by the lender once the loan settles.

Who this guide helps most

This guide is most useful for borrowers who already know the broad goal but need a cleaner decision path before lodging an application. That includes a first home buyer deciding whether deposit size or borrowing power is the tighter constraint, an owner-occupier reviewing a current loan against the market, an investor checking serviceability before the next purchase, and a self-employed borrower needing a realistic discussion about variable income.

It also helps borrowers whose situation overlaps more than one category. A refinance linked to equity release is different from a straight rate review. A purchase linked to a pending sale may raise bridging questions. A build in a growth corridor creates a different sequence from an established-home purchase in an older suburb. In each case, the useful outcome from the first meeting is simple, a document list, the main approval pressure points and the next step from application through to settlement.

  1. Define the purpose. State whether the loan is for a first purchase, refinance, investment, construction, self-employed application or bridging need.
  2. Check the numbers. Bring deposit details, debts, living expenses and income evidence so serviceability can be tested properly.
  3. Compare structures. Ask to see the trade-offs between fixed and variable options, offset features, LMI impact and any equity release.
  4. Confirm disclosure. Ask how the broker is paid and whether any fee for a complex scenario must be disclosed in writing up front.
  5. Map the next step. Leave with a document list and a clear sequence from application to approval and settlement.
