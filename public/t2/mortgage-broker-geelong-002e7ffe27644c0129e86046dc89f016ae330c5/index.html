Matching the review to your borrower scenario

The strongest part of the published guide is that it separates borrower situations instead of treating every enquiry the same. For first home buyers, the review should work through the Victorian First Home Owner Grant, the First Home Guarantee, lenders mortgage insurance, deposit gaps and lender choice before an inspection weekend or offer deadline forces the issue. This is critical because first home buyers often face unique hurdles such as limited deposit savings or the need to understand complex grant conditions.

For a refinance, the stated review points are current rate, fees, offset setup, equity and repayment pressure. A refinance review is not just about finding a lower rate. It is about assessing whether the current loan structure is still serving your financial goals. For example, moving to an interest-only period might free up cash flow now but could impact long-term wealth accumulation. The guide highlights the importance of looking at the whole position rather than just the headline rate.

For an investment purchase, the guide highlights interest-only options, offset, an equity funded deposit and portfolio servicing. Investors need to ensure that the loan structure supports their broader financial strategy. This might involve using an offset account to reduce interest payments or structuring the loan to maximise tax benefits. The review should also consider the long-term servicing capacity of the loan, especially if interest rates rise.

Construction or non-standard income scenarios are also covered. These files can turn on documents and timing rather than just product choice. For construction loans, the review must account for the staged payments and the completion guarantee. For self-employed borrowers, the review needs to verify income stability and ensure that the lender's criteria for non-standard income are met. The guide makes it clear that these complex scenarios require a thorough review of documents and a clear understanding of the lender's requirements.

Tools and fee expectations

The tools are most useful when they are kept in their lane. The borrowing power calculator is presented as a starting point, and the guide says it applies the 3% serviceability buffer that lenders use. It also says a precise, lender specific number comes from live serviceability after documents are reviewed. The calculator gives you a range, but it is not a guarantee of approval. It is a useful tool for understanding your rough capacity before you commit to a full application.

The fee explanation is similarly practical. The FAQ says most residential mortgage brokers in Australia are paid a commission by the lender, so standard home or investment loans typically do not involve a direct fee. It also says some brokers may charge a fee in complex situations, such as small loans or unusual circumstances, and that this should be disclosed upfront. It is important to ask the broker exactly how they are paid and whether any fees apply in your case. This transparency ensures that you are not caught off guard by unexpected costs.

Use the calculator to frame the range, not to assume approval. Ask what documents are needed for a lender specific assessment.
